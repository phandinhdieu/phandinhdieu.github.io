Interview · 1993

Applying Mathematics and Democracy

Phan Đình Diệu, interviewed by Stein Tønnesson
NIASnytt (Nordic Institute of Asian Studies newsletter), 1993, no. 2
This interview was conducted by the historian Stein Tønnesson at Phan Đình Diệu's research institute in Hanoi (September 1992) and published in NIASnytt, the newsletter of the Nordic Institute of Asian Studies, 1993. As it is a third-party publication, the page below gives a summary and a selection of Professor Diệu's own remarks rather than the complete article. The full scanned original is available as a PDF, and a Vietnamese translation appears on the Vietnamese page.

Under the heading “Computers undermine communism,” the interview opens with an idea that had turned out to be the reverse of what many once expected: in the 1960s, some believed computing would strengthen the centrally planned economies by making them more efficient; instead, the microchip helped make decentralised, market economies flourish. Tønnesson presents Phan Đình Diệu as a figure who personifies that tension between computing and communism - a mathematician who set up Vietnam's first computing centre and became one of the country's few tolerated, outspoken critics of one-party rule, and who, before the 1991 Party Congress, had written a petition to the leadership pleading for democracy and suggesting that the Party abandon Marxism–Leninism.

The interviewer's sketch. Born in 1936, Phan Đình Diệu was too young to fight the French and too old to be mobilised against the Americans. He graduated in 1957, taught mathematics, and in 1962 was sent to Moscow for research. Having become a doctor of science, he returned to Hanoi in 1967 - just as the war approached its height. Wanting to be useful, he turned from pure mathematics to computing and set up Vietnam's first computing centre; from 1977 to 1985 he directed the country's Institute of Computer Science, and at the time of the interview he was vice-president of the National Centre for Scientific Research, working in artificial intelligence. Though never a Party member, he served in the National Assembly from 1974 to 1981.

In his own words

Asked how a mathematician came to work on democracy, Diệu placed himself not as a politician but as a scientist and a patriot:

“I am not a politician… I consider myself a patriot with a passion for poor people, and therefore I want to contribute to my country's development. As a scientist, I discovered quite some time ago that Marxism–Leninism had little to offer a country trying to overcome poverty. While studying computer science, systems theory and the problems of modern management, I realised that the socialist model, such as defined in Marxist–Leninist theory, is unsuitable for a society wanting to develop socially, economically and scientifically.”

- Phan Đình Diệu, interviewed by Stein Tønnesson, 1993

On where Vietnam stood, he saw the economic argument as already settled, and the central problem as a contradiction the reformers had not resolved:

“It seems to me that, in the economic field, the socialist model has already been abandoned in Vietnam.”

“The main aspect of today's situation is the contradiction between the ambition to preserve the dictatorship of the party and, at the same time, to develop a free-market economy. This is a new combination. No communist theoretician has ever contemplated such a combination on anything like a permanent basis.”

- Phan Đình Diệu, 1993

Pressed on whether democracy meant something different in Asia than in the West, he refused the distinction, defining democracy in plain, universal terms:

“Democracy is democracy wherever it is applied. The first requirement of a democratic system is respect for human rights and the rights of the citizen… and the rights of the citizen must be respected by the holding of free elections. The key element in a democratic system is the way that leaders are elected. Democratic elections are free, with a secret ballot, and with the right for everyone to stand as candidates. If these requirements are not met, there is no democracy.”

- Phan Đình Diệu, 1993

And on whether he favoured a multi-party system - a question he knew could be read as calling for instability - his answer was characteristically measured: what matters is not the number of parties but the reality of choice.

“The essential thing is not to have many parties, or a ‘multi-party system,’ but to have a real choice. To have a real choice, two parties may be enough - but then there must be a real difference between them.”

- Phan Đình Diệu, 1993

The interview closes with him weighing the different political realities of Asia and the West without conceding that democracy itself should mean anything less in one place than in another - a stance in keeping with the mathematician's habit of insisting that principles hold wherever they are applied.
